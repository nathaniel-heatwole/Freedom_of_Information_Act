IMPACT

• Successfully obtained 95% of the audio record – now seeking the entirety, and the video record.

• Audio record allowed the unmarried partner of the person who died in the disaster to finally know his cause of death (smoke inhalation) – giving her great solace (23 years after the disaster).

• Preparing article for peer-reviewed scholarly journal on near-death privacy rights, contradictions in its application by federal agencies, and experiences of our FOIA coalition.

BACKGROUND

• Station Nightclub fire (2003): illicit pyrotechnics ignited flammable acoustic foam (100 deaths).

• Causal factor in the occurrence of the disaster was inadequate ATF oversight of federally-licensed fireworks manufactures and suppliers and failure of federal controls on pyrotechnics.

• Goal: obtain from the ATF an audio recording from the fire (forensically recovered by ATF), and the video of the fire.

SUMMARY

• FOIA litigation consultant to McWatters v. ATF.

• Helped frame strategy and legal arguments, researched data and information that contradicts the government's theory of the case, and secured affidavit from subject matter expert.

• Wrote document encapsulating public interest arguments supporting release of the records (FOIA.pdf).

PUBLIC ACCOUNTABILITY

• ATF is the primary federal agency tasked with keeping these dangerous pyrotechnic devices out of the hands of non-licensed pyrotechnicians and out of venues such as the Station Nightclub.

• ATF processes for detecting and preventing deliberate misuse of its systems by bad actors were either ineffective or nonexistent.

• Records document (uniquely) the depths of the horrors that can result when the special trust placed in those government personnel who are tasked with fire safety and protection is violated.

• The NIST investigation report on the fire made no recommendations directed at ATF, nor any recommendations related to federal controls of pyrotechnics (the heat source that caused the fire).

• Release of the records would encourage greater vigilance and proactivity by ATF and NIST, and the development of additional fire safety recommendations, policies, and preventative measures.

PUBLIC SAFETY

• ATF used the audio to understand this particular fire event, but never for the wider purpose: creating generalizable knowledge to improve fire safety.

• Audio record contains information about survivability in fires and emergency egress behavior (both key inputs to risk analysis and related risk management and policy-making).

• Overly conservative survivability criteria drain limited economic resources that could be better spent, while inadequate safety margins expose the public to unacceptable risk.

• By time-correlating the data on the records to the parameters of the fire environment (temperature, heat flux, and oxygen), the survivability criteria could be evaluated and their probabilistic component elucidated.

• Put forth several direct quotes from the fire safety literature, saying the field does not anticipate ever having data like that contained on this audio record.

PRIVACY CONCERNS

• Critically examined assumptions underlying survivor's privacy: that it would cause pain to surviving family members (by reviewing empirical case studies of the impacts), and that transcripts are sufficient for public accountability (by detailing the inherent limitations of transcripts).

• Examined the privacy and data protection practices used by federal agencies in other areas, revealing that the federal government has deemed this level of uncertainty to be adequate in other areas.  

• Uncovered contradictions in federal agency application of the survivor's privacy doctrine, by comparing the release outcomes for these records to those from other accident and disaster events.
